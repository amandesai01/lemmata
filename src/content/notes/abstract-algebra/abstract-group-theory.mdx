## Groups

A group is the algebraic record of symmetry. Whenever a collection of transformations can be composed,
undone, and contains the transformation that does nothing, the collection is a group, and its
properties can be studied without reference to what is being transformed.

<Definition n="1.1">
A **group** is a set *G* with a binary operation (*g*, *h*) ↦ *gh* satisfying three axioms:

1. *associativity:* (*gh*)*k* = *g*(*hk*) for all *g*, *h*, *k* ∈ *G*;
2. *identity:* there is *e* ∈ *G* with *eg* = *ge* = *g* for all *g* ∈ *G*;
3. *inverses:* each *g* ∈ *G* has some *g*<sup>−1</sup> ∈ *G* with *gg*<sup>−1</sup> = *g*<sup>−1</sup>*g* = *e*.
</Definition>

If in addition *gh* = *hg* for all *g*, *h* ∈ *G*, the group is **abelian**. The number of elements of
*G* is its **order**, written |*G*|.

<Example n="1.2">
The integers ℤ under addition; the residues ℤ/*n*ℤ under addition modulo *n*; the non-zero rationals
under multiplication; the symmetric group *S*<sub>*n*</sub> of all permutations of \{1, …, *n*\} under
composition, of order *n*!; and the invertible *n* × *n* real matrices GL<sub>*n*</sub>(ℝ) under matrix
multiplication.
</Example>

<Example n="1.3">
The six symmetries of the triangle in [Fig. 1](#fig-1-caption) form a group *D*<sub>3</sub>, the
operation being “one after the other”. It is not abelian: reflecting in the red axis and then the blue
does not give the same result as the blue and then the red.
</Example>

<Proposition n="1.4">
The identity of a group is unique, and each element has exactly one inverse.
</Proposition>

<Proof>
If *e* and *e*′ are both identities then *e* = *ee*′ = *e*′. If *h* and *h*′ are both inverses of *g*,
then *h* = *he* = *h*(*gh*′) = (*hg*)*h*′ = *eh*′ = *h*′.
</Proof>

## Subgroups

<Definition n="2.1">
A subset *H* of a group *G* is a **subgroup**, written *H* ≤ *G*, if *H* is itself a group under the
operation of *G*.
</Definition>

<Proposition n="2.2" name="Subgroup test">
A non-empty subset *H* of *G* is a subgroup if and only if *gh*<sup>−1</sup> ∈ *H* for all *g*, *h* ∈ *H*.
</Proposition>

<Proof>
A subgroup clearly has this property. Conversely, choose *h* ∈ *H*. Taking *g* = *h* gives *e* ∈ *H*;
taking *g* = *e* gives *h*<sup>−1</sup> ∈ *H*; and then *g*(*h*<sup>−1</sup>)<sup>−1</sup> = *gh* ∈ *H*,
so *H* is closed. Associativity is inherited from *G*.
</Proof>

<Definition n="2.3">
The **order** of an element *g* is the least *n* ≥ 1 with *g*<sup>*n*</sup> = *e*, if there is one. The
**cyclic subgroup** generated by *g* is ⟨*g*⟩ = \{*g*<sup>*k*</sup> : *k* ∈ ℤ\}, and its order is the
order of *g*.
</Definition>

The subgroups of *D*<sub>3</sub> are the trivial subgroup, three subgroups of order 2 (each reflection
together with the identity), the subgroup of order 3 formed by the rotations, and *D*<sub>3</sub>
itself. No subgroup has order 4 or 5, and the next section explains why none could.

## Cosets and Lagrange’s theorem

<Definition n="3.1">
For *H* ≤ *G* and *g* ∈ *G*, the **left coset** of *H* containing *g* is *gH* = \{*gh* : *h* ∈ *H*\}.
The number of left cosets is the **index** of *H* in *G*, written [*G* : *H*].
</Definition>

<Lemma n="3.2">
The left cosets of *H* partition *G*, and each has exactly |*H*| elements.
</Lemma>

<Proof>
Every *g* lies in *gH*, so the cosets cover *G*. If *gH* and *g*′*H* share an element, say *gh* =
*g*′*h*′, then *g* = *g*′*h*′*h*<sup>−1</sup> ∈ *g*′*H*, so *gH* ⊆ *g*′*H*; by symmetry the two cosets
are equal. Finally *h* ↦ *gh* is a bijection from *H* to *gH*, with inverse *x* ↦ *g*<sup>−1</sup>*x*.
</Proof>

<Theorem n="3.3" name="Lagrange">
If *G* is finite and *H* ≤ *G*, then

<Display>|*G*| = [*G* : *H*] · |*H*|.</Display>

In particular the order of a subgroup divides the order of the group.
</Theorem>

<Proof>
By Lemma 3.2, *G* is the disjoint union of [*G* : *H*] cosets, each of size |*H*|.
</Proof>

<Corollary n="3.4">
In a finite group the order of every element divides the order of the group. A group of prime order is
cyclic, generated by any element other than the identity.
</Corollary>

The converse of Lagrange’s theorem fails: a group need not have a subgroup of every order dividing its
own. The alternating group *A*<sub>4</sub>, of order 12, has no subgroup of order 6 (Problem 8.6).

## Normal subgroups and quotients

<Definition n="4.1">
A subgroup *N* ≤ *G* is **normal**, written *N* ⊴ *G*, if *gNg*<sup>−1</sup> = *N* for every *g* ∈ *G*;
equivalently, if every left coset *gN* equals the right coset *Ng*.
</Definition>

When *N* is normal, the cosets of *N* form a group *G*/*N*, the **quotient** of *G* by *N*, under the
operation

<Display>(*gN*)(*hN*) = *ghN*.</Display>

Normality is exactly what makes this well defined: the product must not depend on which elements are
chosen to name the two cosets.

<Proposition n="4.2">
Every subgroup of index 2 is normal.
</Proposition>

<Proof>
If [*G* : *H*] = 2, the two left cosets are *H* and its complement *G* ∖ *H*, and so are the two right
cosets. Hence *gH* = *Hg* for every *g*.
</Proof>

<Example n="4.3">
The rotations form a subgroup of index 2 in *D*<sub>3</sub>, so they are normal, and the quotient has
order 2: it records only whether a symmetry turns the triangle over. The subgroup formed by the identity
and the reflection in the red axis is not normal, since conjugating that reflection by a rotation gives
a reflection in another axis.
</Example>

## Homomorphisms

<Definition n="5.1">
A **homomorphism** is a map φ : *G* → *K* between groups with φ(*gh*) = φ(*g*)φ(*h*) for all *g*,
*h* ∈ *G*. Its **kernel** is ker φ = \{*g* ∈ *G* : φ(*g*) = *e*\} and its **image** is
im φ = \{φ(*g*) : *g* ∈ *G*\}. A bijective homomorphism is an **isomorphism**, written *G* ≅ *K*.
</Definition>

<Proposition n="5.2">
The kernel of a homomorphism is a normal subgroup of its domain, and φ is injective if and only if its
kernel is trivial.
</Proposition>

<Proof>
If *k* ∈ ker φ and *g* ∈ *G* then φ(*gkg*<sup>−1</sup>) = φ(*g*) *e* φ(*g*)<sup>−1</sup> = *e*, so
*gkg*<sup>−1</sup> ∈ ker φ; the subgroup test does the rest. And φ(*g*) = φ(*h*) exactly when
*gh*<sup>−1</sup> ∈ ker φ.
</Proof>

<Theorem n="5.3" name="First isomorphism theorem">
For any homomorphism φ : *G* → *K*, the map *g* ker φ ↦ φ(*g*) is a well-defined isomorphism

<Display>*G* / ker φ ≅ im φ.</Display>
</Theorem>

<Example n="5.4">
The sign of a permutation is a homomorphism *S*<sub>*n*</sub> → \{±1\}. For *n* ≥ 2 it is onto, and
its kernel is the alternating group *A*<sub>*n*</sub> of even permutations, so by Theorem 5.3
*S*<sub>*n*</sub> / *A*<sub>*n*</sub> ≅ \{±1\}.
</Example>

## Group actions

<Definition n="6.1">
An **action** of a group *G* on a set *X* is a map *G* × *X* → *X*, written (*g*, *x*) ↦ *g* · *x*,
such that *e* · *x* = *x* and *g* · (*h* · *x*) = (*gh*) · *x* for all *g*, *h* ∈ *G* and *x* ∈ *X*.
The **orbit** of *x* is *G* · *x* = \{*g* · *x* : *g* ∈ *G*\}, and its **stabiliser** is
*G*<sub>*x*</sub> = \{*g* ∈ *G* : *g* · *x* = *x*\}.
</Definition>

<Theorem n="6.2" name="Orbit–stabiliser">
If a finite group *G* acts on *X*, then for every *x* ∈ *X*

<Display>|*G* · *x*| · |*G*<sub>*x*</sub>| = |*G*|.</Display>
</Theorem>

<Proof>
The stabiliser is a subgroup, and *g* · *x* = *h* · *x* exactly when *g*<sup>−1</sup>*h* ∈
*G*<sub>*x*</sub>, that is, when *gG*<sub>*x*</sub> = *hG*<sub>*x*</sub>. So points of the orbit
correspond one to one with left cosets of the stabiliser, and Lagrange’s theorem finishes the count.
</Proof>

<Example n="6.3">
*D*<sub>3</sub> acts on the three corners of the triangle in [Fig. 1](#fig-1-caption). The orbit of any
corner is all three corners, and the stabiliser of a corner has two elements: the identity, and the
reflection in the axis through that corner. Indeed 3 · 2 = 6.
</Example>

## The Sylow theorems

Lagrange’s theorem limits which subgroups can exist. Sylow’s theorems go the other way: for prime powers
they guarantee that subgroups exist, and they constrain how many there are.

<Theorem n="7.1" name="Sylow">
Let |*G*| = *p*<sup>*a*</sup>*m*, where *p* is prime and does not divide *m*. Then

1. *G* has a subgroup of order *p*<sup>*a*</sup>, called a **Sylow *p*-subgroup**;
2. any two Sylow *p*-subgroups are conjugate in *G*;
3. their number *n*<sub>*p*</sub> satisfies *n*<sub>*p*</sub> ≡ 1 (mod *p*) and *n*<sub>*p*</sub> divides *m*.
</Theorem>

<Example n="7.2">
Every group of order 15 is cyclic. Here *n*<sub>3</sub> divides 5 and is 1 modulo 3, so
*n*<sub>3</sub> = 1; and *n*<sub>5</sub> divides 3 and is 1 modulo 5, so *n*<sub>5</sub> = 1. Each Sylow
subgroup is therefore normal, the two meet only in the identity, and *G* is their direct product
*C*<sub>3</sub> × *C*<sub>5</sub> ≅ *C*<sub>15</sub>.
</Example>

## Problems

<Problems>
1. Show that a group in which *g*<sup>2</sup> = *e* for every element *g* is abelian.
2. Prove that every group of order 4 is isomorphic either to *C*<sub>4</sub> or to *C*<sub>2</sub> × *C*<sub>2</sub>.
3. Find every subgroup of ℤ/12ℤ, and draw the lattice they form under inclusion.
4. Let *G* be a finite group acting on a finite set *X*. Prove that the number of orbits equals the average, over *g* ∈ *G*, of the number of points fixed by *g*.
5. Prove that a group of order *p*<sup>2</sup>, with *p* prime, is abelian.
6. Show that the alternating group *A*<sub>4</sub> has no subgroup of order 6.
</Problems>
